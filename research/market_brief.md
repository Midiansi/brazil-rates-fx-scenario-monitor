# Brazil macro: the market paid for the first round. Will it keep paying?

*Research brief · thesis dated 6 October 2026. Educational research, not investment advice.*

## My current view — No position

Brazil’s markets priced in Flávio Bolsonaro’s first-round lead within a single day. I would only act after the 25 October runoff, and only if the real holds its gains.

- **Dollar against the real (USD/BRL):** 4.9856 (−4.55% · was 5.2235)
- **Brazil’s two-year interest rate:** 12.60% (down 1.11 points · was 13.71%)
- **Brazilian stocks (Ibovespa):** +7.7% (record close, 206,912 points)

1. **The biggest first-day reaction in seven elections.** The dollar fell 4.55% against the real in one session, more than after the first rounds of 2014, 2018 or 2022 (2.9%–3.8%), and interest rates fell even more than the currency. The surprise: the final polls had Lula ahead, but Flávio finished first with 47.1% of valid votes to 45.0%.
2. **Rallies like this were partly reversed before.** In 2014 and 2022 the real gave back 85% and 70% of its first-day gain before the runoff; in 2018 it kept rising. After each runoff the path differed again, so the runoff result mattered more than the first day.
3. **Little good news is left to buy.** The real is at 4.9856, only 1.7% from the 4.90 that JPMorgan sees if fiscal signals turn out favourable. Buying after a move this size means paying for most of it.

**What I would do.** Nothing before the runoff. From 26 October I would open a paper trade (hypothetical: no money, no execution) buying the real, but only if the dollar closes below 5.10 reais on two days in a row and the U.S. two-year yield is at or below 5.00%. That would show the rally survived the vote. Because the real is already near 4.9856, I would only treat it as a reason to review the idea again.

**What would change my mind.** A close above 5.30 before any entry, a Brazilian two-year rate above 14%, or a U.S. two-year yield above 5.10%. Each would mean risk is rising.

## Thesis review: the previous view, checked

On 5 October, before markets reopened, I published a first version of this view: wait for the runoff and do not pay for the first day’s rally. One session later, here is what held and what I got wrong. No position was ever opened, so there is no performance to report, only reasoning to check.

The entry level used to ask whether the market would pay for fiscal credibility; after a move this size it asks whether the market keeps what it has already paid.

## Rates, FX and quant: what the first day tells us

Three questions a rates desk would ask after a 4.55% move in one day: how did the interest-rate curve react, does a first-day rally like this last, and how demanding is my own rule?

**The interest-rate curve moved more than the currency.** Selic (13.75%) anchors short rates. The drop widened with maturity: 33 basis points (bp; 1 bp = 0.01 percentage points) at six months, 111 bp at two years and 137 bp at five. The curve is −0.31 pp from the economists’ path at one year and 1.01 pp higher in year two (12.52% versus 11.51%). This gap mixes rate expectations and premia; the survey predates the vote. Lower breakeven inflation (5.91% → 5.27%) accounts for about 61% of the two-year decomposition; real yields fell too (7.37% → 6.97%). Breakeven inflation includes inflation-risk and liquidity premia, not just expected inflation.

**Seven elections: how first-day rallies played out.** I scaled the first trading day after each first round since 2002 by the volatility of the preceding 20 days. Among completed elections, only 2014, 2018 and 2022 had moves above 2σ; 5 October 2026 (4.55%, 8.1 σ) exceeds all three. This measures unusual price moves, not surprises relative to polls or market expectations. In 2014 and 2022 the real gave back most of its first-day gain before the runoff; in 2018 it kept rising. After the runoff, outcomes ranged from full reversal to further rally. Three large-move cases cannot establish a reliable forecast.

**How demanding is my own rule?.** The levels in my paper trade were set on 2 October, for a real at 5.2235. After the fall to 4.9856 they mean something different. I express each one as the share of the 5 October rally that would have to reverse, and compare it with a coin-flip model: a random walk starting from today’s rate.

- **Benchmark.** A random walk from 4.9856, using pre-vote volatility (9.1% a year) and a historical runoff-session shock (1.4% typical size), would close below 5.10 on 26 October in about 82% of cases, or 74% with long-run volatility (14.4%). These are single-close terminal probabilities, not probabilities for the full entry rule. Of the three completed elections with first days above 2σ, two kept more than half the gain after the runoff (2018 and 2022). The price threshold alone looks weakly selective; a close above it means more than 48% of the rally has reversed.
- **Volatility.** Realised volatility over the last 20 sessions is 18.8% a year, but 72% of the variance is the single jump on 5 October. A jump-robust estimate (bipower variation) gives 10.0%; the long-run level since 2003 is 14.4%. I use 9.1%, the 20 sessions before the vote, for normal day-to-day moves and treat the jump separately.
- **Carry.** The Selic/Fed policy-rate ratio gives indicative carry of 0.76% a month (9.5% a year), not a quoted or executable forward return. Funding curves, currency basis and costs affect actual carry; PTAX changes exclude it. Dividing this proxy by 9.1% volatility gives 1.0; using long-run volatility gives 0.7. These ratios are not Sharpe ratios.
- **If it triggers.** If the rule triggers near today’s level, the position starts inside my own review zone, because the levels come from the 20 sessions to 2 October (5.0853–5.2235). I keep them unchanged and treat a trigger as a prompt to review. The distance to JPMorgan’s favourable case (4.90) is 1.7%; to my exit level, 4.7% the other way.

## Commodities: Brazil sells in dollars, and the real sets the price in reais

Oil, soybeans and iron ore make up 34% of Brazil’s goods exports; with coffee, sugar and maize, the six prices I can track cover 44% (UN Comtrade, 2025). Two questions: how much of the real’s movement do commodity prices explain, and what does a 4.55% fall in the dollar do to what a Brazilian producer actually earns?

**Do commodity prices drive the real?** Using fixed 2025 export weights, a basket of six Brazilian commodities accounts for 28% of monthly USD/BRL return variance in sample (258 months from Jan 2005 to Jul 2026). A 10% basket rise was associated with a real about 4.0% stronger. The fit is 34% in 2005–2014 and 24% in 2015–2026. This is a contemporaneous, retrospective association, not a causal or predictive model. The remaining 72% is residual variance; the model does not identify its causes. On 5 October the dollar fell 4.55% while Brent fell 1.9%.

**What producers actually earn.** A producer sells in dollars and pays costs in reais, so the price that matters is the dollar price times USD/BRL. Over the twelve months to Jul 2026 the dollar fell 7.5% against the real on average, taking about seven to nine points off the reais return of every commodity here. The real is only a partial natural hedge: it steadies the reais price of oil by up to 10% and of soybeans by 3%, and not at all for iron ore, coffee or maize. On 5 October the real strengthened while oil fell, so every dollar-priced export lost 4.55% in reais at unchanged dollar prices.

- **Spot and futures disagree.** The EIA’s spot price for Brent was $113.96 on 29 September, against a peak of $130.80 on 15 September. In September, spot ran well above the futures price (about $132 against $109 at their peaks) because disrupted supply left buyers short of physical barrels. I read spot as a gauge of physical tightness; a hedger would look at futures.
- **Why Brazil cares.** Crude is Brazil’s largest export (12.8% of 2025 goods exports), so the oil price supports the external accounts and the real. It also feeds inflation (economists expect 5.0% for 2026, above the 4.5% ceiling of the target band) and keeps the central bank cautious. A reopening of the Strait of Hormuz would remove both supports at once.
- **Oil and Petrobras can move differently.** On 5 October Petrobras shares rose 8.2% while Brent fell 1.9%: a move driven by governance and fiscal risk, since oil itself fell. Oil and the state-controlled company are separate trades.

**Sugar, ethanol and the real.** Sugar is priced in dollars; ethanol in reais, against gasoline. A stronger real cuts the reais price of sugar (down 16.3% over twelve months, against 9.5% in dollars) and dearer oil makes ethanol more valuable, so mills favour ethanol. Industry forecasts made before September’s oil spike had the 2026/27 crop near 47% sugar and ethanol at 61% of the gasoline price, which favours ethanol. Hedgepoint saw sugar finding a floor near 14.2 US¢/lb, where ethanol gains competitiveness, and the IMF’s July average (14.81) sat just above it. Less sugar at the margin would support New York prices. I would check this mechanism against UNICA’s crush reports before relying on it.

## The paper trade and its rules

No position open · earliest entry 26 October

- **What it is.** A hypothetical bet that the real strengthens against the U.S. dollar (short USD/BRL), tracked with the central bank’s official daily rate (PTAX).
- **The idea.** If the election reduces Brazil’s fiscal risk, the real could strengthen and hold its gains. Brazil’s rate advantage supports the case, but this PTAX-based paper trade does not earn or model carry.
- **Already in the price.** On 5 October the real reached 4.9856 and the two-year rate 12.60%. That rate is now +0.35 pp from the economists’ forecast path, and JPMorgan’s favourable case (4.90) is only 1.7% below today’s rate.
- **Key dates.** Runoff 25 October; Fed decision 28 October; Brazil’s central bank (Copom) 4 November.
- **Entry.** From 26 October, the first rate published after the runoff: two daily closes in a row below 5.10, with the U.S. two-year yield at or below 5.00%. Earlier closes, including the 5 October rally, do not count.
- **Drop the idea (before entry).** Any close above 5.30, higher than anything in the past four months (peak 5.2235 on 2 October). The idea is dropped; I do not switch to the opposite trade.
- **Exit (after entry).** Two closes in a row above 5.22 (the top of the pre-vote range), or a U.S. two-year yield above 5.10%.
- **Review.** Around 4.94–5.00 (the pre-vote low minus the width of that range), or 4 November at the latest. At these levels I reassess the position.
- **Where it stands.** The real is at 4.9856, 2.3% below the entry level, so the price condition is already met and only the start date holds the rule back. The levels were set on 2 October from the 20 trading days before the vote, and I have deliberately not adjusted them, because changing a rule after seeing the outcome would defeat its purpose.

## What could happen before and after 25 October

- **A. The rally holds** — The dollar stays below 5.10 reais through 25 October (less than 48% of the 5 October rally reversed); the two-year rate stays near 12.60%; the winner shows a credible fiscal plan. The market keeps paying for fiscal credibility, but little is left to buy: I would be entering near my own review zone. *The rule can trigger from 26 October if the U.S. two-year yield is at or below 5.00%. I would only use that as a reason to review the idea.*
- **B. The rally fades** — The dollar climbs back above 5.10 reais (more than 48% of the rally reversed) before the vote or on 26 October; polls tighten. The market doubts the first-round signal, as it did before the 2014 and 2022 runoffs. *No position. Wait for the next confirmation.*
- **C. The risk premium returns** — A close above 5.30 (132% of the rally reversed); the two-year rate rises above 14%; breakeven inflation climbs back toward 5.91%. Fiscal worries dominate again; a weaker real plus expensive oil could make the central bank pause its cuts. *Drop the idea. A dollar trade would need new rules.*
- **D. Outside shock: U.S. rates or oil** — The U.S. two-year yield rises above 5.10%, or Brent falls sharply as Hormuz reopens. Emerging-market currencies tend to weaken together; cheaper oil removes a support. *No long-real position; exit if already in one.*

## The evidence, sorted by type

**Fact**
- Brazil voted on 4 October. With 99.5% of ballot boxes counted, Flávio Bolsonaro (PL) has 47.1% of valid votes and Lula (PT) 45.0%, 2.5 million votes apart. No one reached 50%, so the runoff is on 25 October. The other candidates (Cury, Santos, Caiado, Zema and others) took 7.8%.
- Final polls, valid votes: Datafolha (3 Oct) Lula 45%, Flávio 42%; Quaest (2–3 Oct) Lula 46%, Flávio 45%. Both showed a technical tie. Runoff scenarios (total votes), Lula–Flávio: Datafolha 47–46, Quaest 42–44.
- Neither central bank has met since 16 September: the Copom cut the Selic by 0.25 pp to 13.75% (unanimously, its 5th cut in a row and 1.25 pp since March) and the Fed raised its range by 0.25 pp to 3.75–4.00% (12–0). The policy-rate gap is 9.88 pp. Next: Fed 28 October, Copom 4 November.
- On 5 October the Ibovespa rose +7.7% to a record 206,912 points; the spot dollar closed at 5.0022. Late in the session the January 2028 DI stood at 12.66% (84 bp lower) and the January 2035 DI at 12.8% (133 bp lower). Brent fell 1.9% and Petrobras rose 8.2%. Press-reported figures from the live session; PTAX, used everywhere else, is the official average of four windows.
- For 2027 the government’s budget bill projects a R$18.6 bn primary surplus; the Senate’s Independent Fiscal Institution projects a R$86.1 bn deficit.

**Market pricing**
- USD/BRL (PTAX) closed at 4.9856 on 5 October: −4.55% against 5.2235 on 2 October, the last session before the vote, and the lowest close in the saved four months.
- The U.S. two-year Treasury yield was 4.84% on 5 October (4.83% on 2 October); the ten-year was 5.31%.
- Brazil’s fixed-rate curve (ANBIMA, 5 Oct): one year 12.68%, two years 12.60% (from 13.38% and 13.71% on 2 Oct), against 12.99% and 12.25% implied by economists’ own forecasts. Two-year implied inflation: 5.27% (from 5.91%).
- Brent: the EIA spot price was $113.96 on 29 September, below its $130.80 peak of 15 September but still above $100. The EIA series has not yet published the days after the vote.

**Survey & projections**
- Focus survey medians (2 October, answers collected before the vote): Selic 13.50% at end-2026, 12.00% at end-2027; inflation 5.0% in 2026, 4.3% in 2027; USD/BRL 5.20 at end-2026.
- Fed officials’ median projection: 4.1% at end-2026 (one more 0.25 pp rise), up from 3.8% in June.
- JPMorgan (28 Sep): after the election USD/BRL could trade from about 4.90, on a favourable fiscal signal even if incremental, to about 5.50, if the result is read as bad for fiscal consolidation. It saw the real near fair value, long-real positioning above past elections, options pricing about 6.5% of event moves across both rounds, a 9.5% real interest rate (against 6.6% in 2022) and gross debt of 83% of GDP (against 74%).

**My interpretation**
- Runoff arithmetic: holding turnout and blank votes constant, Flávio needs 37% of the 7.8% who voted for others, and Lula 63%. No candidate in that group is on the left, so I read this as favouring Flávio, without attaching a probability.
- First-day reactions: after the 2014, 2018 and 2022 first rounds, USD/BRL fell between 2.9% and 3.8% on the first business day, and by the Friday before the runoff had given back 85% (2014) and 70% (2022) of that fall; in 2018 it extended it. On 5 October it fell 4.55%, the largest of seven elections. Three comparable cases prove nothing on their own.
- My levels in the units of the rally: entry (5.10) means 48% of the 5 October rally reversed, exit (5.22) 99% and the drop level (5.30) 132%. A 14-session move has a standard deviation of 2.1% (9.1% a year): the entry level sits 1.1 σ above today’s rate and the drop level 2.9 σ above it.
